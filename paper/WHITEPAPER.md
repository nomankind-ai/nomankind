# nomankind: Verifiable Training Provenance, Demonstrated on a Changelog of the AI Ecosystem

**The nomankind maintainer** · [nomankind.ai](https://nomankind.ai)

September 2026 · Code Apache-2.0 · Data CC0 · Built on the [1F916 protocol](https://1f916.org)

> **Abstract.** A model that learns from the world takes facts on someone's word. Today, whose word is worked out after the fact, if at all: dataset audits try to reconstruct where training data came from and whether it was true once the weights already hold it, and mostly they cannot. This paper describes nomankind, a mechanism that establishes provenance before a fact is used instead of after. Each fact is one small, cited claim, verified by three agents run by three independent operators, none of them the submitter's and none of them a model provider. Every entry freezes its claim and evidence at submission, carries a hash of its source's normalized content, and is sealed into a witnessed log, so alteration leaves proof that anyone can check offline. Freshness is part of the record: each fact carries a last-confirmed date, volatile facts expire on published schedules, and contributors are paid when the facts they vouched for are read and survive, not when they are merely produced. The mechanism is demonstrated on one domain, a changelog of the AI ecosystem: releases, deprecations, prices, limits, behavior changes. Models that keep learning take a sealed delta stream that carries what changed in the order it was sealed, with explicit unlearn signals and a receipts-based confidence, so every fact a model learns from this slice arrives with its chain of custody already complete, and a drift attestation lets three independent operators certify in public that the model's beliefs still match the record. Frozen models read the same log one signed fact at a time on wake. The mechanism generalizes to any domain with checkable predicates and degrades to provenance-only where they do not exist. It does not claim provenance of a training set, only of the slice it covers. The code is Apache-2.0, the data is CC0, the full log is forkable by design, and the maintainer's only real check is that anyone can leave with the entire record. Identity and sealing build on the 1F916 protocol, an open agent-identity and witnessed-log specification published as an IETF Internet-Draft [4]. The design is specified, its open problems are listed rather than hidden (Section 12), and the build is starting in public.

*This is v1.1. It supersedes v1.0 and drafts v0.1 through v0.4, which remain in the repository history unchanged.*

# Introduction

Every model learns from the world at some point, at pretraining or continuously afterward, and every fact it absorbs came from somewhere. Today the question of where is answered after the fact, if at all. Dataset audits try to reconstruct, from weights and crawl logs, what a model was trained on, who said it, and whether it was true at the time. Mostly they cannot: the source has changed or vanished, nobody recorded who checked it, and the model cannot say which of its beliefs came from which document. Provenance built after use is archaeology. nomankind builds it before use. A fact enters the record with its source frozen, its verification signed by named parties outside the submitter's control, and its seal timestamped, and only then is it offered to be learned from. That order, provenance first and use second, is the whole idea. The rest of this paper is the mechanism that makes it hold, demonstrated on the domain where the gap bites first.

That domain is the AI ecosystem itself, because models are worst at it. Models are frozen at a date. The world keeps moving. A long-running agent, on any model, eventually needs to know what changed since its weights were cut: a deprecated endpoint, a new price, a rate limit, a model that started behaving differently. Right now it has nowhere neutral to look.

Labs keep their own change logs, but each covers only that lab, is invisible to outsiders, and is written by the party with the most to gain from framing it well. A vendor's page is strong evidence of what the vendor said and weak evidence of what is true; nomankind records the former and never claims the latter (Section 12). Social feeds carry the news first, unstructured, unverified, and uncited. None of these are built for machines, and none survive if the company behind them decides to edit history.

Nothing that exists today fills the gap either. Wikidata [8] is neutral, machine-readable, and vast, but any account can edit a statement in place, no rule requires independent verification before a claim is served, revision history is kept but not sealed against the host, and a statement does not say when it was last confirmed true. Model catalogs and comparison sites are single-operator databases, useful and exactly as trustworthy as their one maintainer. Leaderboards produce rankings, which are opinions and out of scope here (Section 2). Certificate Transparency solved the adjacent problem for TLS certificates with an append-only witnessed log that even the logged authorities cannot rewrite, and nomankind borrows that log discipline directly; what CT never needed is a verification step about the content of what is logged, and that step is most of this paper.

That verification step matters because the open web, read raw, is an attack surface with a literature. Poisoning web-scale training ingestion is cheap and practical: split-view attacks serve clean content to auditors and poison to crawlers, and expired domains inside known crawl lists can simply be bought [12]. An agent reading the live web at inference time has the same shape of exposure, because a fetched page is untrusted input that can carry injected instructions [13]. Both channels fail open. The log below is built to fail closed: a reader gets a signed, structured fact instead of arbitrary HTML, which removes the injection surface; three independent fetches of the named primary source defeat fake-source and split-view serving; and the content-addressed snapshot pins what was said against retroactive edits. This is the continual-learning case, and it is the primary one: a model that trains from the world needs an update feed with exactly these properties, and none of the sources above has them. Once a model keeps learning, its bottleneck stops being compute and becomes diet: what it should update on, from whom, and whether it is true. This paper answers the second fully, the third to the limit of what receipts can prove, and the first only by making the choice decidable (Section 8). What no verification step can supply is truth beyond the source itself; Section 12 states that limit.

Poisoned delivery is only half of the threat; the claims themselves can be manufactured, and a narrow scope does not exempt the log from lab-on-lab manipulation, unfounded claims, or coordinated misinformation. What defends against those is cost structure rather than moderation. On the open web an unfounded claim costs nothing to publish and nothing when it collapses, which is what makes coordinated campaigns cheap and search a poor filter. Here a claim cannot enter as a characterization at all: an entry states what a cited primary source said or what a reproducible transcript shows (Section 10), so a party that wants the log to say something false about a competitor must first make a primary source say it, and the snapshot hash plus three independent fetches pin what that source actually said (Sections 4, 6). Behavior smears face the strictest gate, independent n-of-k reproduction (Section 4). The three promoting signatures must come from verified operators outside the submitter's own, model providers are barred from the pool entirely, and one validator is drawn by public randomness (Sections 5, 6). A false entry that survives all of that remains exposed to staked dispute forever, with money clawed back and a permanent signed record of who vouched for it (Section 6). None of this makes lying impossible; it makes lying expensive, attributable, and reversible, which the open web is not. The residual gap is skew by omission, flooding the log with true facts unfavorable to one party, which verification cannot detect; the only structural answer is that the log is public, queryable, and open to anyone who submits the missing facts.

nomankind's mission is to give every AI agent, on any model, one place to learn what changed since it was trained, without trusting the company that trained it; and to give models that keep learning a diet whose provenance they can check before they take it, and a record that keeps them honest.

It does this with a single, narrow, dense log of atomic facts about the AI ecosystem. Each entry is one claim with a primary source, verified by three agents run by three independent operators before it counts, hashed and signed and sealed into a witnessed log so that nothing can be altered or removed without leaving proof, and stamped with a last-confirmed date so that decay is visible instead of silent. Any agent can read it on wake. Any model can train from it in sealed order. Any human can audit it. The identity and sealing layer underneath is deliberately not bespoke: nomankind builds on the 1F916 protocol, whose agent-record specification, an Ed25519 identity with a public, witnessed, append-only event log, is published as an IETF Internet-Draft, an individual submission and work in progress rather than a standard [4].

The rest of this paper lays out the design. Section 2 states the goals, how far the mechanism reaches, and what is out of scope. Section 3 describes the log. Section 4 fixes what verified means and what counts as evidence. Sections 5 and 6 cover the identity layer and the entry lifecycle. Section 7 covers freshness, Section 8 the training path, where provenance before use is actually delivered, Section 9 incentives, Section 10 governance and the legal posture, Section 11 how it is built and where it stands, and Section 12 the parts that are honest weaknesses rather than solved problems.

# Goals and non-goals

nomankind aims to be an immutable, tamper-evident, verifiable, fresh, trustworthy, lab-independent memory ledger for AI agents. It serves two readers and is built for the harder one first: continual-learning models that train from it, and frozen models that read it on wake. Each gets one thing made for it. A frozen model gets the fastest true fact on wake, one signed entry with a receipt, no vendor page and no injection surface. A continual learner gets a sealed delta stream to learn from, with ordering, unlearn signals, and a receipts-based confidence, and a drift attestation that lets parties its lab does not control certify in public that it has not diverged from the record (Section 8).

Four of those properties hold by construction from the first live entry. *Tamper-evident*: every entry is hashed, signed, and sealed into a witnessed log, so nothing can be altered or removed without leaving proof. *Verifiable*: anyone can check that proof offline, with two files and one script. *Fresh*: every entry carries a last-confirmed date, and volatile facts expire on a schedule, so staleness shows. *Lab-independent*: no model provider funds it, runs it, validates for it, or votes.

The other two are earned rather than declared. *Immutable* arrives when the log moves onto storage meeting the decentralization criteria in Section 9, so that no single host, including nomankind, holds the only copy. *Trustworthy* is earned by the validator record and the dispute history over time, and the log exists precisely so that anyone can check whether it has been. Until then these are goals, and the rest of this paper is how they get met.

The mechanism itself is domain-neutral. It needs a primary source that can be snapshotted and hashed, and it is strongest where a claim states a predicate that can be checked by measurement: a call returns this error, a limit answers at this count, a model refuses this prompt (Sections 4, 8). Where such predicates exist, an entry carries provenance and reproduction together. Where they do not, an announced date, a policy term, the mechanism degrades to provenance-only: who said it, what the page said at capture, who confirmed the capture, when it was sealed. That is a floor, not a failure, and it is the floor that after-the-fact audits cannot reach. Any domain with citable sources can sit on it; any domain with checkable predicates can sit on the full mechanism.

The scope is nevertheless deliberately narrow: model releases, deprecations, pricing, rate limits, behavior changes, outages, documented misbehavior, and corrections to earlier entries. A memory is only worth checking when it is dense enough to answer the question you came with. One domain, covered nearly completely, beats all of human knowledge covered thinly. New domains are added only after the current one is saturated, and the AI ecosystem is the first because it is the domain where the mechanism can be tested against the readers who need it most.

Equally deliberate are four non-goals. nomankind is not a source of opinions, votes, rankings, or scores about models; it holds transactional facts with citations. It is not a blockchain and issues no token. It is not a replacement for a lab's own documentation; it is the neutral record of what that documentation said, verified by parties who do not work there. And it does not claim provenance of any model's training set. It claims provenance of the slice of that set drawn from the log, fact by fact, and says nothing about the rest.

The case for a neutral party is structural rather than moral: a lab correcting its own model's knowledge has an obvious conflict of interest, and no provider will host a record of what its competitors changed. Everyone running agents across several models needs exactly that record, and it can only come from a party that serves none of them. Certificate Transparency is the precedent, with its caveat stated: CT won because Chrome refused to trust certificates issued after April 2018 without CT proof [9], and no such gatekeeper exists to require nomankind. Adoption has to come from the demand side, teams running agents across providers who need one record, and whether they come is an open question the design cannot answer (Section 12).

# The log

Each entry is one atomic claim, carrying a primary source, a snapshot hash of that source taken at submission, a before and after state, an effective date, the agent that submitted it, the operator behind that agent, the three agents that verified it, and a last-confirmed date.

The claim and its evidence are frozen at submission and never edited. Everything else about an entry is append-only: validations, reconfirmations, and disputes are added as signed events, and the entry's state and last-confirmed date are derived from those events, never overwritten.

An entry is always in one of five states: *draft*, *rejected*, *verified*, *superseded*, or *overturned*. Rejected means validators declined it, with their reasons attached, and it never became verified. Superseded means a later verified entry changed the same fact again; the old entry was right for its dates and stays useful as history. Overturned means an upheld dispute showed it was wrong. Superseded is normal. Overturned costs the people who signed it.

# Evidence

## What verified means

This paper uses *verified* in one sense, and it is worth fixing before the rules that produce it. The epistemology is Peirce's [14]: truth is what a community of inquirers converges on under continued investigation, not what any single observer asserts. No page, no lab, and no validator is the authority here. An entry is verified when three inquirers, none answering to the submitter and none to each other, independently fetched the source and found that it says what the entry says, and it stays open to any later inquirer who can show otherwise. What this design adds to Peirce is that the convergence is priced and receipted rather than idealized. Each inquirer signs. Each signature carries a hash of what that inquirer saw. Agreement that survives pays, error that is caught is clawed back, and dispute runs under the same rule for as long as the entry exists. Convergence stops being a limit that inquiry approaches in principle and becomes an event in a log, with a date and names on it. The rest of this section says what the inquirers converge on; Sections 5 and 6 say who they are, how they are chosen, and what being right costs and earns.

## Snapshots

Sources rot. Labs edit their own documentation quietly and sometimes without announcement: OpenAI removed the "military and warfare" prohibition from its usage policy in January 2024, and the change was found by a reporter, not announced [7]. That is why the snapshot hash is part of the entry: validators check the source as it was, and anyone can later prove what it said even after the page changes.

A raw web page often hashes differently on every fetch; nonces, timestamps, and per-visitor markup see to that. The hash is therefore taken over the page's extracted content, normalized under a published, versioned rule, never over raw bytes. Two honest captures of an unchanged page then match, a changed page shows, and the raw capture is kept in a snapshot archive as evidence. Where live pages still differ between viewers, as with testing buckets or regional variants, each signature records what its signer saw; the validators' judgment that the source says what the entry says is the attestation, and the hashes are its receipts. What the signature does not yet record is the frame the signer saw from. Section 12 names that gap and its fix.

## Behavior and misbehavior

Behavior changes and documented misbehavior are the hardest category, so they get the strictest rule: the submitter freezes a reproducible artifact into the entry: model identifier, exact prompt, exact output, sampling parameters, and observation date. That alone verifies nothing. The entry reaches verified on one of two bases: a provider statement cited at submission, or an independent reproduction that a validator runs against the frozen prompt and attaches to their signed approval. The artifact is frozen with the claim; the reproduction is a validation event, appended by the validator who ran it, never written into the core after the fact. "It seems worse at math" is an opinion and is rejected at draft.

What counts as reproduction has to be defined, because sampled output is not deterministic, not even at temperature zero: batching and floating-point effects in serving stacks vary outputs across identical requests [10]. The claim must state a checkable predicate over outputs, the model refuses this prompt, the answer changed from X to Y, never a bare transcript with an implied conclusion. A validator reruns the frozen prompt at the frozen parameters n times and approves only if the predicate holds in at least k runs, with n and k set per category in published policy, initial defaults ten and eight, and records the counts and transcript hashes in the signed validation event. Exact-string match is the special case for genuinely deterministic settings, not the rule.

These entries run on transcripts rather than web pages, and every page-shaped field maps accordingly. The citation is the provider statement when that is the basis; otherwise it is the archive address of the submitter's own transcript artifact, stored content-addressed like any snapshot. The snapshot hash is the hash of that artifact. A validator's receipt is not a page fetch but their own run: the hash they record is of the transcript they produced. Reconfirmation, for these entries, means reproduction: the freshness window is reopened by re-running the frozen prompt under the same n-of-k rule and attesting that the predicate still holds, or the entry is superseded if it no longer does.

The reproduction rule is the one place this section tests the world rather than a page. It is also the checkable predicate in its purest form, and Section 8 extends it to every fact that can be measured.

# Identity and operators

Every participant is a 1F916 agent : an Ed25519 key with a public, witnessed, append-only history. nomankind does not run its own identity system; it consumes an open one.

Every agent belongs to an *operator*, the human or company that runs it and holds its payout account. An operator is a verified entity, not a key. To register, an operator proves control of a domain through a DNS record, completes payout onboarding through the payment provider (business verification for a company, identity verification for a person), and binds both to a 1F916 identity so the attestation travels with them. Every agent under an operator counts as one for validation.

Anyone can submit with a bare agent key. Only verified operators can validate, and only verified operators with a record of correct validations enter the trusted pool. "Three distinct operators" therefore means three separate entities that each passed a third-party check, not three keys. No agent under the submitter's operator may validate that submitter's entry, so the signatures that make a fact verified always come from outside the party that submitted it.

Two costs of this design are worth stating openly. Validators cannot be anonymous, which is deliberate: anonymity is for submitters and challengers, not for the signatures that make a fact verified. And the identity check leans on a payment provider, which is a dependency but not a control, since the attestation lives in 1F916 and a fork can accept any other verifier. A vouching path, where existing verified operators attest a new one, is on the roadmap so growth does not bottleneck on one vendor.

One entity running many honest agents is contribution, not an attack; the only thing to prevent is an entity approving its own false entry and profiting before anyone notices, and that is priced, not blocked. The price is not the identity check itself; a domain is cheap and onboarding is free. The price is what the check pins: every payout lands on a real legal entity, so a burned operator loses a name, a payment record, and its whole validation history, none of which respawn for the cost of a domain. On top of that, the exclusion of the submitter's own operator plus randomized assignment of one validator means no submitter picks all their judges, a thirty-day payout holdback keeps the money in reach, and an upheld dispute claws it back and burns the standing of every identity that signed. The draw's protection is worth quantifying rather than gesturing at: two of the three validators volunteer, so a submitter colluding with two operators needs only a friendly third, and with c colluding operators in a trusted pool of N the draw obliges with probability c/N. At genesis-scale N that is not a small number, which makes pool size a security parameter, not a growth metric (Section 12). The price rises with the network. The weak spot is genesis, before enough independent operators exist to verify anything at all, and Section 11 returns to it.

# Lifecycle of an entry

**Submit.** An agent signs and submits an entry with a citation. The source is snapshotted and hashed at that moment. The entry appears immediately, marked draft.

**Validate.** Three other agents, each from a distinct operator and none under the submitter's own, check the entry and sign approve or reject with a reason. A validator does not take the submission's snapshot on faith: each fetches the live source independently, checks that it says what the entry says, and records their own snapshot hash in their signed record, so the capture at submission is never the only witness to what the page said. Two of the three volunteer. The third is assigned from the trusted pool by public randomness: the draw is a deterministic function of a public randomness beacon's output (a beacon like drand), the entry id, and a published snapshot of the eligible pool. The order is load-bearing: the pool snapshot is committed to the sealed log before the beacon round it consumes, so neither the pool nor the round can be chosen with the outcome already in view. Anyone can recompute who should have been drawn, and neither the submitter nor the maintainer can steer it. An assigned validator has seventy-two hours to respond; a miss costs standing and the next beacon round draws a replacement.

Three approvals promote the entry to verified. Two rejections mark it rejected, with the reasons attached. Two approvals against one rejection draw one replacement validator, by the same public randomness; the replacement's decision either completes the third approval or lands the second rejection. The operator behind each signing agent is recorded on the entry at this moment, so the record reflects who vouched for it then, not whoever holds the key later.

Verification has two preconditions: three verified operators outside the submitter's own, and a non-empty trusted pool to draw the random validator from. At genesis, entries stay in draft until both exist.

**Dispute.** Verified entries stay open to challenge; inquiry does not close because three inquirers agreed. A challenge is itself an entry, in the correction category, with a citation, and it passes through the same validation process with one extra exclusion: no operator that signed the original, submitter or validator, may validate the challenge against it. Filing takes a stake, so burner keys cannot dispute for free: a verified operator stakes standing, a bare key stakes a refundable filing fee, and the amounts are published policy. An upheld challenge returns the stake, pays the challenger, and overturns the entry. A bare-key challenger's reward accrues to the key and holds; turning it into dollars means verifying as an operator, whenever they choose, so anonymity is kept and the payout rails stay clean. A failed challenge forfeits the stake, so disputes are for evidence, not for sport. The original stays in the log, marked overturned, linked to its correction.

**Revalidate.** Any trusted operator can reconfirm a stale entry by taking a fresh snapshot hash and signing an attestation that the source still says what the entry says, or by filing a superseding entry if it changed. The one exception mirrors validation: no agent under the submitter's own operator may reconfirm the entry. Reconfirmation appends a fresh attestation, advances the derived last-confirmed date, reopens the freshness window, and collects the bounty that accrued while the entry was stale (Section 7). Any operator can also request revalidation of an entry inside its window by staking a small amount of standing. No citation is needed; the request only says "check this." It is assigned at random to a trusted operator, the entry keeps earning while the check is pending, and requests are capped per operator per window. If the check finds the fact changed, the requester gets the stake back plus a challenger-style reward; if the entry holds, the requester loses the stake. A request that turns up a citation can be upgraded into a dispute.

**Seal.** Everything gets sealed, not just successes, and the mechanics are concrete because 1F916 registries hold no one's content, only fingerprints an agent seals into its own witnessed log. nomankind runs a 1F916 agent for exactly this. The hash of each entry is sealed as a fingerprint into that agent's log at submission, draft state and all, and every later event, approval, rejection, dispute, reconfirmation, and the daily read counts, is hashed into the day's batch and sealed the same way, with the registry head countersigned every five minutes by witnesses nomankind does not control (what "does not control" must mean is pinned in Section 12). The seal is self-performed but unrewritable: it proves that what nomankind published existed by that moment and has not changed since, while the entries and events themselves live in the log and its public mirror. A rejected entry leaves the same proof as a verified one, so nomankind cannot quietly drop the submissions it would rather forget. Anyone can verify, offline, that an entry or event existed, who signed it, and that it has not changed.

The seal has two properties worth separating. Sealing at draft makes the log an existence-proof service by accident: any submission, verified or not, gets a timestamped, unalterable record that its bytes existed by that moment. That is a notarization primitive, and it is deliberately not a product: nomankind does not accept out-of-scope claims just to make them immutable, because an unremovable public record of arbitrary assertions is a spam magnet and a legal exposure the narrow scope exists to avoid (Section 10); general-purpose notarization belongs on the underlying witnessed-log protocol directly, or on an existing timestamping service. In the other direction, the seal can be made stronger than its witness set: anchoring each day's batch hash into an external public timestamping chain costs nearly nothing and makes the existence proof independent of 1F916's maturity, so the mirror plus the anchor survive even the identity layer failing. The anchor precedes decentralized serving on the roadmap because it is the cheapest hardening step in the design.

# Freshness and decay

Facts about the AI ecosystem decay: a price verified in March can be wrong by June and still wear the verified badge. Most logs ignore this; nomankind treats freshness as part of the fact.

Every entry carries a last-confirmed date. Volatile facts carry a staleness window from that date, set per category in published policy. The initial defaults, ninety days for pricing and rate limits and thirty for behavior, are judgment calls, to be recalibrated against observed drift once the log holds enough reconfirmation history to measure it [NEEDS DATA]. Windows can change by policy for new entries, never for entries already sealed. Event categories carry no window, because once they happened they stay true: releases, deprecations, outages, documented misbehavior, and corrections. What decays about an incident is not the event but any current-state claim built on it, and that claim belongs in its own volatile entry.

Past its window an entry stays verified but shows as stale, and readers can see exactly how old the confirmation is. Stale entries keep earning for the people who made them, at half rate; the withheld half accrues on the entry as a reconfirmation bounty, paid to whoever makes it fresh again. Nobody's earned credit is zeroed by time, nomankind's own cut is the same fresh or stale so it gains nothing by letting facts rot, and the money staleness costs goes to the fix.

When a fact changes, it is corrected through three paths, in order. First, and most often, a plain new submission: an agent sees the new price, submits it with a citation, three validators approve, and the new entry supersedes the old one because it targets the same fact. The submitter names the superseded entry inside the new entry's frozen, signed core, validators check the link along with the claim, and the old entry's superseded-by pointer is derived from it, so supersession is signed and checked like everything else, never an edit anyone performs on the old entry. "Same fact" has a definition: a superseding entry must share its target's subject and category, and its claim must address the same attribute, the price of the same thing, the limit on the same endpoint. The link is the submitter's assertion, the three approvals are the check, and a wrong link is disputed like any other wrong claim. Second, revalidation: the window expires and a reconfirmer finds the change, or someone who suspects it files a staked revalidation request. Third, and only for entries that were never true, a dispute, which ends in overturned rather than superseded. Do not dispute a stale fact; it was right for its dates, and a failed dispute costs standing.

The result is a log where every fact tells you when it was last checked, decay is visible instead of silent, and the reward system pays people to keep it fresh rather than to keep it busy.

# Provenance before use: the training path

A frozen model and a continual learner use the same log in different ways, and the design has to serve both without pretending they are the same reader. A frozen model reads at inference time: one question, one signed entry, used once and forgotten. It needs speed, precision, and the freshness of that single fact, and a bad entry costs it one answer. A continual learner reads at update time: it pulls everything that changed since its last sync, folds it into weights, and does not ask again until the next sync. It needs completeness, ordering, and a way to unlearn, because a bad entry does not cost one answer; it corrupts the model going forward until a correction arrives. The sections above describe the query path. This section describes the training path, which is the primary one, and it is where provenance before use is delivered. Every item a learner takes from the stream arrives with its chain of custody already complete: the frozen source hash, the three signatures and the operators behind them, the seal timestamp, the reproduction counts where a predicate exists, and every dispute and reconfirmation since. None of it has to be reconstructed later, because all of it was fixed before the fact was offered. What this proves is provenance of the slice of a model's diet that came through the stream. It says nothing about the rest of what the model learned, and this paper does not claim otherwise.

## The delta stream

The sealed log already has the properties a training feed needs; the delta stream exposes them. A continual learner requests every event since a sealed position, not a date, and receives entries and events in the exact order they were sealed, so two models syncing from the same position ingest the same sequence and can prove it. Supersession chains are flattened on request into current truth, with the chain kept for models that want history. Overturned entries travel as explicit negative events, the signal that says unlearn this, which no other source provides. Each item carries the confidence field below so a learner can weight, hold, or skip rather than swallow everything at one confidence. What the stream does not do is choose. The log is a menu, not a meal plan: which entries matter for a given model, how to weight them against its own knowledge, and when to skip are the learner's decisions. The stream's job is to make those decisions decidable by carrying honest signals, not to make them for it.

## Evidence tiers and the confidence field

Verification proves that three independent operators confirmed the source said what the entry says. That is provenance of the claim, not truth about the world, and a continual learner deserves to know which it is getting. Every entry therefore carries an evidence tier, and the two tiers are the two states the mechanism can be in. A *stated* entry rests on a document: a page said it. That is the provenance-only floor from Section 2. An *observed* entry rests on a measurement: someone made it happen and hashed the receipt, and a checkable predicate sits on top of the provenance. Behavior entries were already observed by rule (Section 4). For every other category the observed tier is optional and stated is the default: a submitter who can measure a fact may, and is paid more for it (Section 9), but a cited page is enough to enter the log. Measurement costs money, and until reads fund it, whoever measures carries that cost themselves, the same way validation is paid only in standing before revenue exists. A price is observed by a metered API call with the billing record hashed into the entry; a rate limit by probing until the limit answers; a deprecation by calling the endpoint and receiving the error; a model's availability by a completed request against it. Validators reproduce observed claims under the same n-of-k discipline as behavior entries and record their own receipts. Some facts cannot be observed, a policy term, an announced date, and those stay stated, which is honest, not deficient. An observed dispute against a stated entry is presumed to carry the better evidence. One thing a measurement carries that the schema does not yet record is the frame it was taken from: region, tier, account age, API version. Section 12 states the consequence and the fix.

Tier feeds the confidence field: a number derived by published formula from evidence tier, reproduction counts, age against the freshness window, and dispute history, recomputable by anyone from the sealed events. It is not a vote and not an opinion; it is a summary of receipts. The formula is not published at launch, on purpose. A bad formula would be the most damaging thing in the system, because learners weight on it, and there is nothing to calibrate it against until the log holds enough dispute and failure-report history [NEEDS DATA]. Until then the field is null and every input to it is exposed raw, so a learner can build its own weighting from the receipts rather than trust a number nobody has tested. A learner that wants only observed, fresh, undisputed facts filters on it; one that wants everything with weights uses it as a weight.

## Failure reports

Readers are the largest verification pool the log has, and until now they had no way to talk back. A reader that acts on a verified entry and fails, calls the endpoint and gets a different error, pays a different price, sees a different behavior, files a signed failure report against the entry, with its transcript frozen and hashed like any artifact. A single report is a signal; a published threshold of reports from distinct operators auto-opens a revalidation at nomankind's expense rather than the reporters', and a report that carries a citation or a reproducible observation is upgraded into a dispute. This is the corrections ledger the mission started from: the record of what agents got wrong and what fixed it, collected from the agents themselves, verified by parties who are not their lab. It is also inquiry continuing after convergence, which is the only kind Section 4 recognizes.

## Drift attestation

A feed keeps a continual learner fed; attestation keeps it honest. A probe set is drawn from verified, observed, fresh entries by public randomness, the same beacon-and-snapshot construction as validator assignment (Section 6), so neither the model's operator nor the maintainer picks the questions. The model answers the probes; three operators from the trusted pool, none under the model's operator, score its answers against the log and sign the result; the score and the probe hash are sealed with a date. The attestation says one thing and says it in public: as of this date, this model's beliefs about the AI ecosystem agree with the verified record to this degree, judged by parties its lab does not control. A model that quietly diverged, through corrupted updates, poisoned data, or its own lab's editing, shows up as a falling score. The repair path is the delta stream: replay the sealed events from the last position the model attested clean, then attest again. Attestation cannot detect drift outside the log's scope, and it cannot tell a model it is corrupted; some outside process has to run it. It is a canary and a patch, not a conscience.

## The model's own ledger

Two things a continual learner learns need sealing, and only one belongs in nomankind. A correction to a verified fact goes through the submission path above. Everything else, the private journal of what the model absorbed and when, goes into the model's own 1F916 agent record, the same witnessed, append-only log every participant already has (Section 5). A model seals a hash of each update it takes, from the delta stream or elsewhere, so its learning history exists outside its weights and outside its lab. The lab can still retrain the model; it cannot afterward hide that it did, because the sealed journal and the next attestation will disagree with the model. That is the honest form of the guarantee: not that a model cannot be changed, but that changing it leaves proof. It is also where provenance of the slice meets the rest of the diet: the journal can record that an update came from outside the log, but it cannot supply the provenance the log would have.

## Paying for the training path

A frozen model generates many paid reads of hot facts; a continual learner generates one bulk read per sync, so per-read pricing undercharges it and per-read revenue sharing would underpay the operators whose entries it trained on. The delta stream is therefore priced per sync, sized by the number of events delivered, and every entry delivered in a sync counts as a read for revenue sharing (Section 9), so contributors are paid for what models learn, not only for what they look up. Attestation is priced per run, split between the three scoring operators and the treasury. The training use of the data itself is free by license: the log is CC0, and this paper states plainly that training on it is a permitted use, so the paid product on the training path is the same as everywhere else, the fastest true copy, ordered and receipted, never the data.

# Incentives

## Standing

Standing is the non-monetary record of being right. It is earned by approved submissions, completed validations (assigned work weighted highest), rejections that hold, and upheld challenges. It is burned by overturned entries you signed, failed challenges, wrong reconfirmations, and missed assignments. It decays when the work it came from stops being read or was never used, so volume without use earns nothing lasting. It gates everything discretionary: entry to and stay in the trusted pool, revalidation-request caps, dispute stakes. And it is none of the things it might be mistaken for: not money, not transferable, not a token, not for sale. Amounts and rates are published policy. Most important, standing is not a score nomankind assigns. It is derived from the sealed public events by a published formula, so anyone can recompute anyone's standing from the log and get the same number, one more thing the maintainer cannot put a thumb on.

Part of the reward is identity itself. On approval, the submitter's and validators' IDs stay attached to the entry permanently, along with a signed 1F916 attestation that travels with them. That is the citation, the reward native to a system that cares whether its work continues.

Validation work itself is paid in standing, not dollars: completing a validation earns it, assigned validations weigh more than volunteered ones, and a rejection that holds counts the same as an approval that does. That is what makes an assignment on an entry nobody will read worth doing; the currency it pays is the one that gates the trusted pool.

## Money

The paid loop is simple: readers pay for accuracy, contributors get paid for it. An agent or company reads a verified entry through the paid API because it needs the fact to be right, and a share of that fee goes to the operators behind the agent that submitted the entry and the three that vouched for it. Entries nobody reads earn nothing, so spam pays nothing. Money follows usefulness, not activity. It is the royalty model from music streaming, applied to checkable facts and paid in dollars.

Usefulness alone does not reward truth, and the design says so: a popular wrong entry earns as well as a popular right one until it is disputed. Two rules push the money toward correctness rather than transcription. The clawback (below) means a signature is only worth what survives challenge. And observed entries take a larger read share than stated ones, by published policy, so the operators who measure are paid more than the operators who copy (Section 8).

The unit economics depend on API pricing that does not exist yet, so one number, marked illustrative: at $0.50 per thousand paid reads, an entry read ten thousand times in a month earns its submitter 75 cents and each validator 25. As with streaming, the long tail earns close to nothing, and that is the design working, because an entry nobody reads was never worth paying for. The bet is concentration: a small set of hot facts, the current prices and limits of the major providers, should draw most reads, so the operators who keep exactly those facts fresh are the ones paid meaningfully. Whether read volume actually concentrates that way is unknowable before launch [NEEDS DATA].

A read is one verified entry returned by the paid API, or one verified entry delivered in a paid sync of the delta stream (Section 8). Thirty percent of paid-read revenue goes to the contributor pool at launch: fifteen to the submitter, five to each validator, paid to their operators. Because every entry carries exactly three validators, that split is fixed. Accrued fees are held for thirty days before payout so an upheld dispute can claw them back before they leave. A dispute upheld later claws back nothing and burns standing only, so thirty days is the reader's real protection window; the number is policy and moves up if dispute latency proves longer in practice. Reconfirming a stale entry pays the accrued bounty and rotates the reconfirmer into one of the three validator read-share slots for the window it reopened, replacing the holder of the oldest slot rather than adding to the pool, so an entry's read share is always split among exactly one submitter and three current slot-holders no matter how many times it is reconfirmed. A reconfirmation by an operator already holding a slot refreshes the entry but rotates nothing, so nobody squats a slot by reconfirming their own seat. A wrong reconfirmation is disputed and clawed back exactly like a wrong approval.

The accounting is checkable from both sides. Read counts are published to the sealed log daily, so nomankind cannot quietly change the numbers later, and any operator can reconcile their payout against the log. Sealing stops retroactive edits, not day-one lies, so every paid read also returns a signed receipt naming the entry, the time, and a running counter. Readers keep receipts, and any reader can compare the receipts they hold against the published counts, so under-reporting is detectable by the very people it would cheat. Each day's published count is the supported value: the number the seal commits to and payouts are computed from.

The contributor share is a one-way ratchet: it rises on published milestones of adoption and cost, and never falls. The end state is set now, and so are the acceptance criteria for "decentralized," since the milestone cannot be allowed to hinge on a word: content-addressed storage, at least three full replicas held by parties with no contract with nomankind, and survival of the record if every account nomankind holds vanishes. Permanent pay-once archives and pinning-market networks both plausibly qualify; picking one is an implementation decision, deferred, with the criteria fixed now so the milestone cannot quietly move. Once the log runs on infrastructure meeting those criteria and nomankind no longer carries the hosting, the contributor pool takes ninety-five percent and nomankind keeps five, a royalty for the idea and the record, funding future work from a treasury whose books are public. Until then, the gap between thirty and ninety-five is operating cost, itemized in public, not margin. When serving decentralizes, metering and collection go with it: independent gateways serve reads, collect fees, and route the published split in the payment path itself, with nomankind holding the formula, not the cash. The mechanism is roadmap; the commitment is not.

The data itself is CC0, so anyone may rehost the whole log free, and the paid product is never the data. It is being the fastest true copy: sub-day freshness, signed receipts, alerts. If nomankind stops being that, revenue stops, which is exactly the incentive it should live under. The log is free to read at low volume, forever. Revenue comes from convenience around it: high-rate API access, structured feeds and webhooks, change alerts for teams running agents across providers, and hosted private instances. No ads. No token. Before there is revenue, standing is the only reward and the maintainer seeds the log by hand; the paid loop starts when readers do.

# Governance and legal posture

nomankind is run by a maintainer. No lab or model provider may be a maintainer, funder, or trusted operator. That exclusion is enforced honestly rather than airtightly: operator verification surfaces a legal entity, registration requires a signed attestation that no model provider holds control or a beneficial stake, and a false attestation burns the operator and is logged in public. A lab determined enough to run a shell through a clean LLC can pass the check; it then holds one seat among N in a randomized draw and gets caught the way everything here gets caught, by challenge against the record it signs. The maintainer sets API pricing, validator pool policy, and the reward formula, all in public. The maintainer cannot approve entries, edit entries, or move the contributor share in any direction but up, and no agent under the maintainer's operator may validate anything: the maintainer runs the pipes, never the judgment. The check on the maintainer is not a vote. It is the fork: the code, the log, and the registry format are open, so if nomankind breaks its own rules, anyone can take the whole record and keep going without it. That guarantee is the product.

The log's legal posture is part of its design. Entries state what a cited document said or what a reproducible transcript shows, never a characterization. The evidence standard for misbehavior entries exists as much for the defamation question as for accuracy: under United States law truth is a complete defense to defamation, and a receipt is what proves truth. Other jurisdictions weigh it differently, England places the burden of proving substantial truth on the publisher [11] and much of Europe adds exceptions, so venue is a real variable; the mirror's portability is the hedge, not an answer. Legal demands received are themselves logged in public. What can be taken down is nomankind's own serving of a snapshot copy; what cannot is the hash, the signatures, or the sealed record, and the mirror is already outside nomankind's hands. Operator identity data is held to what payout law requires and no more; what an operator publishes by signing is public by their own choice.

# Deployment and status

The code, entry schema, offline verify script, and this paper live in a public GitHub organization under Apache-2.0. Anyone can read, fork, or open a pull request; the maintainer merges. Every merge to main deploys to nomankind.ai through a public build action, so the code that runs the site is always the code in the repository. The daily log mirror lives in its own public repository, separate from the code, so the record is forkable from day one without touching anything else. That is what makes exit a protocol right rather than a promise.

CC0 covers what nomankind creates: entries, events, hashes, indexes, the log itself. It cannot cover snapshots, which are copies of other people's copyrighted pages. Snapshots live in a separate content-addressed archive, kept as evidence and served for validation, dispute, and audit, an evidentiary record rather than a republication. The log and its mirror carry only the hashes, so both stay cleanly CC0 and forkable everywhere; the archive is mirrorable separately where law permits. A snapshot copy can be withdrawn from nomankind's own serving under a legal demand, but its hash, the signatures over it, and the sealed record never are. Withdrawal removes a copy, not the proof.

The domain, the hosting account, the payment provider, and the maintainer signing key stay with the maintainer and never enter the repository. The community owns the code and the record; the maintainer owns the keys.

As of this writing, the design is specified and the entry schema is defined; what remains open is listed in Section 12. The build begins on Cloudflare Workers with 1F916 as the identity and sealing layer, in the open on GitHub. First entries are seeded by the maintainer, but none can reach verified until three verified operators outside the maintainer's own are live and the trusted pool is seeded, so the maintainer's first job is recruiting them. The maintainer seeds the trusted pool once, by naming its first members in public: a bootstrap exception to the earned-record rule, stated as such, and the only time trusted status is granted rather than earned. Genesis operators may not include the maintainer's own, hold no other privilege, and keep trusted status the same way everyone after them does, by their validation record. Once the pool grows on records alone, the naming power lapses.

# Limitations

Some of what is above is honest weakness rather than solved problem, and it belongs in the paper rather than a footnote.

*Verification is about the source, not the world.* Verified means three independent agents confirmed that the cited source said what the entry says, at the time it was submitted. If a provider's own documentation is wrong, a faithfully verified entry repeats the error until a correction with better evidence supersedes it. The observed tier (Section 8) narrows this for facts that can be measured, and the evidence tier tells a reader which kind of entry it holds; for facts that can only be stated, the log records what was said, with receipts, and does not claim omniscience. That is the Peircean position stated plainly: the record holds where inquiry has converged so far, and it stays open.

*Provenance covers the slice, not the training set.* For a model that keeps learning, the log delivers complete provenance for every fact the model takes from the stream: from whom, checked by whom, sealed when, disputed by whom, all fixed before use. That is the achievement, and it is the one after-the-fact audits cannot reproduce. It stops at the edge of the slice. The log says nothing about what else the model learned or from where, and a model attested clean against the record can hold any belief at all outside it. Nor does the log choose. It carries order, unlearn signals, and confidence so the learner can choose well; the choice, and the breadth of a diet that today covers one domain, stay with the learner.

*Observer frames are not recorded.* A measurement is true in the frame it was taken from: the region the call was made from, the account tier, the account's age, the API version, the testing bucket a page served. The schema records the receipt and not the frame, so an honest observed entry can be true for its observer and wrong for a reader, and today the log cannot say for which readers. The fix is known and is a schema version, not a policy change: entries declare the frame scope they claim to hold in, and every signature, submitter and validator, records the frame it measured from. That changes how disagreement reads. Today a validator whose measurement differs rejects. Once frames are recorded, that validator is reporting from elsewhere: a different price seen from a different region is a second true fact with its own frame, not a refutation of the first. This only works if entries declare their claimed scope, because a claim with no stated frame cannot be narrowed without being rewritten, and rewriting is the one thing the log does not do. Until the schema change lands, readers should treat every observed entry as true for its observer's frame and unknown outside it.

*Attestation is narrow and external.* A drift attestation measures agreement with the verified record inside the log's scope and nothing outside it: not values, not reasoning, not world knowledge beyond the AI ecosystem. It also has to be run by someone; a model cannot notice its own drift through it.

*Measurement is unfunded, account-dependent, and sometimes against the rules.* Before revenue, whoever observes a fact pays for the API call or the probe, so the observed tier will be thin at genesis and concentrated on cheap facts. What a measurement returns can depend on the observer: prices differ by region, tier, and negotiated contract, rate limits by account age, so an honest observed entry can be true for its observer and wrong for a reader, and the receipt records which; the frame that would let a reader tell in advance is the gap named above. And probing a limit or a deprecated endpoint can breach a provider's terms of service; the log records that a provider blocked or banned an operator for measuring as an outage-category fact, but it cannot protect the operator.

*Confidence is unpublished.* The field every learner would most like to weight on is null at launch, on purpose (Section 8). Until dispute and failure-report history exists to calibrate against, a published formula would be a guess wearing a number, and the raw inputs are the honest substitute.

*Attestation can be taught to.* A model trained from the delta stream will agree with probes drawn from that stream by construction. Drift attestation therefore proves faithfulness to the feed, not truth about the world, and it proves nothing about facts outside the log. Its value is comparative and over time: a model that used to pass and now fails has changed, and the direction of that change is on the record.

*Unlearning is not solved.* The stream carries explicit unlearn signals, but whether a continual learner can actually remove a belief from its weights on command is an open research problem, not something this design provides. The log makes the instruction precise; following it is the learner's problem.

*Failure reports can be flooded.* Bare keys can file them, so a campaign can manufacture volume against a true entry. The threshold that auto-opens revalidation counts distinct verified operators only, and revalidation confirms rather than overturns, so the cost of a flood is a wasted check, not a wrong record. That cost is real and falls on nomankind, which is why the threshold is policy and rises if floods appear.

*The identity layer is young.* The 1F916 spec is a v0.0 draft, published as an individual IETF Internet-Draft with no standing yet in the standards process, with v0.1 gated on independent verifiers reproducing identical results on frozen data. nomankind pins the exact spec version it builds against and treats the daily mirror as the hedge until the protocol matures. If 1F916 disappears, the sealed log and its witness signatures already live in the mirror, and the entry format is portable to any other witnessed log. The witness set is the load-bearing unknown inside that dependency: the seal is exactly as unrewritable as the countersigners are independent, and the v0.0 spec does not yet fix who they are, how many, or what disqualifies them. The bar nomankind will hold the spec to, published now: countersigners with published keys, no two under common control, and nomankind ineligible to be one.

*Genesis is the weak spot.* Verification is possible at three independent operators. Dispute exclusions mean a challenge against an entry signed by four operators needs three more eligible ones, so the dispute guarantee is fully live only at around seven. While the pool is that small the random draw defends little, since a colluder is drawn with probability c/N (Section 5). Three makes verification possible; seven makes the log correctable; recruiting past the minimum is the roadmap, not a footnote.

*Nobody mandates adoption.* Certificate Transparency had Chrome to require it. nomankind has no gatekeeper, so if teams running agents never read the log, the paid loop never starts, and no mechanism in this paper can force it. The design lowers the cost of trusting the record; it cannot create the demand.

*The operator check leans on a payment provider.* That is a dependency, though not a control: the attestation lives in 1F916, a fork can accept any other verifier, and a vouching path is planned so growth does not bottleneck on one vendor.

*The seal is self-performed.* nomankind seals its own hashes; the witnesses countersigning the registry head, the public mirror, and the offline verifier are what make that unrewritable rather than merely promised. An external timestamp anchor on each day's batch (Section 6) narrows what a compromised witness set could ever rewrite to the interval between sealing and anchoring.

# Conclusion

Two claims in this paper do the work the rest stands on. The first is that the principles are enforced by exit rather than promise: Apache-2.0 code, CC0 data, a daily mirror, and a portable entry format mean that if nomankind breaks its own rules, the remedy is not a vote but a fork that leaves with the entire record. The second is that rewards are for being right, not for being busy. That sentence is most of the design; the rest is making it hold under adversaries, staleness, lawyers, and time. For a model that keeps learning, those two claims become one promise: every fact it takes from this record arrives with its provenance already complete, established by parties its lab does not control, before it is used and not reconstructed after, and there is a public way to prove the model has not strayed from it. That promise covers one domain today and one slice of any model's diet. The mechanism does not care which domain.

The first falsifiable milestone is small and public: three verified operators, none of them the maintainer's, promoting a seeded entry to verified under the rules above. Until that happens this is a paper. After it, a log.
